First timers tips on attending a church

What actually happens at a service

Services vary, but most follow the same shape. Here's the run of it.

Arriving

Someone will probably greet you at the door and hand you a bulletin or an order of service. In some churches that's an usher in uniform; in others it's whoever is nearest. If nobody greets you, walk in and find a seat. You don't have to introduce yourself to anyone.

Music

Most services open with singing, anywhere from ten minutes to an hour. Some people stand, some close their eyes, some raise their hands, some dance. Stand or sit as you prefer. You don't have to sing. Nobody is watching.

Announcements and readings

Community notices: events, prayer requests, welcomes for visitors are read during the service. Some churches read parts of the service aloud together, with the words in the bulletin or on a screen.

The sermon

The main teaching. Twenty minutes in some churches, well over an hour in others. You listen, that's all. In plenty of places people call out "Amen" while the preacher talks.

The offering

As a visitor you are under no obligation. Pass it along. Sometimes this is a long ceremonial moment with music and strong encouragement to give. Sometimes you barely notice it happened. Either way, you don't have to give anything.

Communion

Bread and wine or juice. Catholic Mass centres on it and takes it weekly; many Protestant churches do it monthly or quarterly. Some churches welcome everyone, some have rules about it. If you are unsure, watch what others do or sit it out.

Prayer

In some churches one person prays briefly from the front. In others the whole room prays aloud at once, which sounds like chaos and isn't.

Afterwards

Usually coffee, sometimes a full meal. You can leave as soon as the service ends. If you stay, someone will introduce themselves.

How long

Anywhere from 45 minutes to four hours. Check the website before you go. You may leave during the service.

What to wear

Wear what you would wear to something that mattered: clean and presentable. Past that, it depends on the church.

Some congregations are jeans and trainers, pastor included. In others, dressing well is part of the celebration and people arrive in their best. A few have firm expectations about modesty, head coverings or shoes off at the door.

Look at the church's social media before you go. Photos from recent services tell you the dress code, along with the energy, the age range and the general feel of the place.

Bi- and Multilingual churches

Plenty of services run in two or three languages. A church may hold one service in its first language in the morning and another at midday. If you can't follow the words, watch the congregation for when to stand, sit or respond. Tone carries a lot. You can usually tell prayer from celebration from grief without understanding a syllable. Ask quietly and someone will often translate for you as it goes. Larger churches sometimes have translation headsets, so it is worth asking at the door.

Some traditions keep an ancient language in parts of the liturgy: Ge'ez, Coptic, classical Greek, Church Slavonic, Latin. That isn't an obstacle to sit through. It's centuries of continuity, and it's worth hearing on those terms.

If you want an English or a different language service, most church websites list the languages they use. In non-English-speaking cities, look for international churches or large city-centre congregations.

When services happen, and how long they last

Large churches often run three or four services on a Sunday, sometimes starting as early as 6am. Many hold a midweek service that is as full as the Sunday one. Catholic parishes offer Mass several times on Sunday and often daily.

Length varies more than anything else. Some services run 45 minutes, some run four hours, and the people consider both perfectly normal. Check before you go so you can plan your day.

Arriving late is fine almost everywhere. In longer services people come and go throughout. In a small church you'll be noticed, but that's never a reason to stay home.

Check times on the website or call ahead. They move for holidays and special Sundays, and nothing is more deflating than an empty building.

Online church

Many churches transitioned to online broadcast in 2020 during the global pandemic and most never stopped. You can watch a livestream from your sofa, catch up on a sermon later in the week. The limitation is that watching alone isn't the same as being in a room with people. The conversations afterwards, the practical help, the being known. Most people eventually want some version of the physical thing.

If you are starting online, watch a few different churches before you settle. You have access to the whole world from your sofa. Use it.

Will anyone pressure me

Mostly, no. Churches do want you to come back.

Things that might feel strange but aren't

  • Being asked to stand and be welcomed as a visitor. It is celebration.
  • Being handed a gift bag, booklet or food. Take it. You owe nothing in return.
  • Being asked to fill in a visitor card. You don't have to. If you do, expect a call or an email.
  • Someone offering to pray for you. "No thank you" is fine
  • An altar call, where people are invited forward at the end.
  • Crying, raised hands, dancing, speaking in tongues. You don't have to join in.
  • The whole room praying aloud at once. It sounds like chaos and it means a great deal to the people doing it.

What different churches may feel like

Churches differ in energy and structure more than most people expect. These are broad groupings, and any individual church may not match its label.

Liturgical

Catholic, Anglican, Lutheran and others. The same structure every week, more or less wherever you are in the world: readings, set responses, communion. You can follow along in the book or the bulletin.

Orthodox

Greek, Russian, Coptic, Ethiopian, Eritrean, Syrian. The oldest continuous form of Christian worship. Incense, icons, chant, long services, often standing. Immersive, and unlike anything else on this list.

Pentecostal and charismatic

The fastest-growing movement in Christianity and now the dominant form in much of the world. High energy, long services, a lot of prayer, room for the unplanned.

Evangelical and non-denominational

A band, casual dress, a long sermon, a short service. The most common style in Britain and America.

Historic Black church traditions

Baptist, AME, COGIC and others. Expressive, musical, deeply communal, and among the most significant institutions in American life.

Quaker

Near-total silence, no clergy, anyone may speak if moved to. Radically different from everything above.
